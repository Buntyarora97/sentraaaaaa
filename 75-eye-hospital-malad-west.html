What Happens at Your First Visit?

  1. Tell us the real problem. Explain when the symptom started, whether it affects one or both eyes, and what makes it better or worse. Mention diabetes, blood pressure, thyroid disease, past injuries and previous operations.
  2. Start with vision and examination. The team checks visual acuity and prescription, then examines the eyelids, cornea, lens and eye structures. Eye pressure or dilation may be recommended when clinically appropriate.
  3. Complete only relevant tests. If the examination indicates cataract, retinal disease, glaucoma, corneal irregularity or another condition, the doctor explains why a scan or imaging test is useful.
  4. Discuss choices in plain language. You receive a diagnosis or working diagnosis, what can wait, what should not wait, and what the next step is. Surgical decisions include risks, benefits, alternatives and expected recovery.
  5. Leave with a plan. Ask about drops, activity restrictions, warning symptoms, follow-up and whom to contact if your vision changes suddenly.

Bring sunglasses if dilation is possible. You may also want someone to accompany you if bright light or temporary blur would make travel difficult. The purpose of a good first visit is not to create anxiety; it is to replace uncertainty with a practical plan.

Why Follow-Up Is Part of Hospital Quality

A procedure is one moment in a longer care journey. Before surgery, the team checks that the diagnosis is correct and that the plan is suitable. Immediately afterwards, the patient needs clear drop instructions and a way to report an unexpected symptom. During follow-up, the doctor checks healing and vision, answers practical questions and decides when the next review is needed. This continuity is particularly important for patients travelling from Malad West because it avoids confusion about where to go after reaching home.

Ask these questions before you leave any hospital after treatment:

  • What medicines should I use, how often and for how many days?
  • When is my next review, and does the timing change if the eye feels worse?
  • Which activities should I avoid temporarily—driving, dust, swimming, lifting or screen work?
  • Which symptoms are expected and which symptoms need an urgent call?
  • Who should a caregiver contact if the patient cannot explain a sudden change?

Good instructions are specific enough to follow at home. If you are unsure, repeat them back to the team. It is better to ask one extra question in the clinic than to worry about a drop schedule later in the evening.

📝 Caregiver tip: use a written medicine timetable, keep the prescribed drops separate from old bottles, and never share eye drops between family members.
