What Eye Flu Is (And Isn't)

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva — the transparent membrane covering the white of the eye and the inner surface of the lids. Three completely different causes produce similar-looking symptoms but require completely different treatment:

🦠

Viral (Eye Flu)

The epidemic form that sweeps Mumbai every monsoon. Watery discharge, gritty feeling, often with mild cold symptoms (runny nose, mild fever). HIGHLY contagious — spreads through hands, towels, surfaces. Self-limiting in 1–2 weeks. The type for which steroid drops are most dangerous.

🔬

Bacterial

Thicker yellow-green discharge, lids stuck shut in the morning — the classic "woke up can't open my eyes" presentation. Responds to antibiotic drops. Less contagious than viral but still requires hygiene precautions and its own treatment — lubricants alone won't clear it.

🌸

Allergic

ITCHING is the giveaway — intense, constant, both eyes simultaneously, seasonal or triggered by dust/pollen/pet dander. NOT contagious. Treated with antihistamine drops and allergen avoidance. Steroid drops work but are overused — long-term steroid without supervision has real risks.

The type decides the treatment — which is precisely why chemist-counter combinations (typically antibiotic + steroid mixtures) so often fail or harm. One antibiotic won't fix viral conjunctivitis; one steroid can make herpes conjunctivitis sight-threatening.

Do's — The First 48 Hours

  • Cold compresses for comfort — a clean cool cloth on the closed eyes relieves the burning and helps reduce swelling. Refresh every 20 minutes.
  • Preservative-free lubricant drops as advised — wash the surface debris and reduce irritation. Frequency as needed for comfort.
  • Wash hands relentlessly — before and after touching the eye, before applying drops, after handling anything the patient has touched. Hand hygiene is the single most effective infection-control measure.
  • Separate towel, pillow cover, and handkerchief for the patient — these items transmit the virus directly. Wash separately in hot water.
  • Dark glasses for light sensitivity and courtesy in public — reduces photophobia discomfort and signals to others without explaining.
  • Isolate essentials: no shared eye makeup, no shared phone-to-face contact, children home from school while discharge is active (check school policy).

Don'ts — Where Every Season's Damage Happens

🚫 Critical Don'ts — Read These First

  • NO steroid drops without a doctor's examination. This is the single most damaging self-medication practice in Indian eye care. Steroid drops on the wrong type of conjunctivitis — particularly herpes simplex viral keratoconjunctivitis — can cause permanent corneal scarring and even vision loss. They feel better initially (they suppress inflammation) and catastrophically worsen the underlying infection. Do not use the "neomycin + steroid" combinations sold freely at Mumbai chemists without a diagnosis.
  • No rubbing. Rubbing spreads the virus from one eye to the other and from your eyes to your hands to every surface you touch. The one you rubbed is also now more inflamed.
  • No contact lenses until fully recovered — the lens sits on an inflamed cornea, traps infection, and extends the illness. Remove them at first symptom.
  • No kajal/surma "treatment" — applying kajal to infected eyes worsens bacterial load. We still see this regularly.
  • No rose water — rose water is not sterile; it introduces bacteria onto an already compromised surface.
  • Don't share the family's one eye-drop bottle — the bottle tip itself becomes a vector, transmitting infection to every family member who uses the same bottle.

Red Flags — See a Doctor the Same Day

Simple viral eye flu is self-limiting in 1–2 weeks with correct hygiene. These red flags indicate a not-simple case that requires same-day clinical examination:

😰

Pain

Discomfort and grittiness are expected; PAIN (beyond surface irritation) is not. Pain in conjunctivitis should prompt same-day evaluation — it suggests corneal involvement, which changes the diagnosis and treatment entirely.

💡

Significant Light Sensitivity

Mild light sensitivity is common. Significant photophobia — where normal daylight or indoor light is genuinely painful — suggests corneal involvement (keratitis), which needs urgent treatment to prevent scarring.

👁️

Vision Change

Any reduction in vision, blur that doesn't clear with blinking, or a white spot visible on the cornea — come the same day. Vision loss is never a feature of simple viral conjunctivitis.

📅

Worsening Beyond Day 4–5

Viral conjunctivitis peaks at day 3–5 and then begins improving. Worsening after this point suggests secondary bacterial infection, corneal involvement, or a different diagnosis entirely.

👶

Special Populations

Eye flu in a contact-lens wearer, newborn, or immunocompromised patient (diabetes, HIV, cancer treatment) is ALWAYS examined — never self-treated. These groups face significantly higher complication risk.

⬜

White Spot on Cornea

A visible white opacity on the cornea during an eye flu episode is a corneal ulcer until proven otherwise — a medical urgency requiring same-day evaluation and treatment.

Protecting the Rest of the Family

The patient is contagious while the eye is actively watering and discharging — roughly the first 5–7 days. Here is the practical household protocol:

  • Dedicate a separate towel, pillow cover, and face cloth — retire them to a separate hot-water wash after the illness
  • Disinfect phone screens, door handles, tap handles, and kitchen surfaces daily — the virus survives on surfaces for hours
  • The patient handles their own drops; no one else uses the same bottle
  • If a second family member develops symptoms, they need their OWN new bottle of drops — not the first patient's
  • Work-from-home during the contagious week if possible; if not, obsessive hand hygiene and no shared equipment
  • Eye makeup used during the illness is discarded after recovery — mascara, kohl, and liner can harbour the virus

Frequently Asked Questions

Aankh aana kitne din mein theek hota hai?

Viral eye flu aam taur par 1–2 hafte mein khud theek hota hai — ilaaj aaram aur complications se bachav ke liye hota hai. Bacterial conjunctivitis antibiotic drops se 3–5 din mein theek hota hai. Allergic conjunctivitis recurring hoti hai — baar baar theek hoti hai baar baar aati hai, jab tak allergen exposure control na ho. Dard, roshni se takleef ya nazar kam hona ho toh usi din jaanch karayen.

Does looking at someone with eye flu spread it?

No — this is the most persistent myth in India about conjunctivitis and it is completely false. Eye flu spreads through direct contact: hands touching infected surfaces, then touching your own eyes; shared towels, pillowcases, and eye makeup; and shared eye-drop bottles. Looking at someone — maintaining eye contact — does not transmit the virus. The reflex to avoid eye contact with infected patients is socially embarrassing for them and provides no protection for you.

Which drops should I buy for eye flu?

Don't self-select — especially avoid steroid-containing combinations sold freely at chemists. On the wrong type of infection (particularly herpes), steroid drops cause permanent corneal damage. Preservative-free lubricants are safe for comfort; everything else should follow a proper examination. The chemist doesn't know whether you have viral, bacterial, or herpes conjunctivitis — neither can you, without a slit-lamp.

Can I go to office with eye flu?

Ideally not while the eye is actively watering and discharging — the contagious week. You will seed the office. Work from home if possible. If you must attend, obsessive hand hygiene, no touching your face, no sharing equipment, and disclosure to colleagues so they can take their own precautions.

Ek aankh mein shuru hua, doosri mein bhi aa gaya — yeh kaise?

Aksar haath se — aankh ragda, phir doosri aankh chhua. Viral conjunctivitis both-eye spreading ka sabse common reason haath se transfer hai, न ki "kuch aa gaya". Isliye rubbing bilkul band karo aur haath dhona consistently karo.

Is eye flu dangerous for contact-lens wearers?

More so than for non-wearers — contact lenses trap infectious material against the cornea, increase bacterial load, and significantly raise the risk of corneal ulceration. Contact lens wearers should remove lenses at the FIRST symptom of any eye infection and not replace them until fully recovered and the eye has been examined. Do not try to "just wear them less."
